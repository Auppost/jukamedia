How to Write a Service Page That Brings Enquiries

October 19, 2026·8 min read

When someone searches for "boiler repair in Tallinn" or "accountant for small business", they rarely land on your homepage. They land on a single page about one service, and that page has a few seconds to answer one question: "Can this company solve my problem?" Many small business websites squeeze all their services into one paragraph on the homepage, or give each service a page that says little more than "we offer high quality at a fair price". Both waste the visit. A dedicated, well-built service page is one of the cheapest ways to turn search traffic and ad clicks into real enquiries.

1. One page, one service, one intent

Each service that people search for separately deserves its own page. A visitor looking for "kitchen renovation" is not helped by a page that also covers bathrooms, roofs and garden fences. A focused page matches what the visitor typed, reads as expert, and gives Google a clear topic to rank. It also makes Google Ads simpler: you can send each ad group to the page that matches it, which usually improves both relevance and conversion.

2. Start with the outcome, not with your history

The top of the page, before any scrolling, should state the service, the audience and the result in plain words. "Bathroom renovation for apartments in Tallinn, finished in 3 to 4 weeks" says more than "Welcome to our company, established in 2009". Add one clear button (call, message or request a quote) right there. Your company history belongs further down or on the About page, where visitors who already trust you can read it.

3. The four questions every service page must answer

Almost every visitor, in some order, is silently asking the same things. Make the answers easy to find:

  • What exactly do you do? Describe what is included and, just as useful, what is not included.
  • Is this for me? Name the type of customer, property or situation you handle best, and the area you serve.
  • What will it cost? A price, a "from" price or a typical range filters out the wrong enquiries and builds trust. If prices truly vary, explain what they depend on.
  • What happens next? A short numbered process (enquiry, quote, work, handover) removes the fear of the unknown.

4. Proof beats promises

Claims like "professional team" and "individual approach" are on every site, so nobody believes them. Replace them with things a visitor can verify: a short case with real before-and-after photos, a named review with a link to your Google profile, the number of years or projects, certifications, or a warranty period. One concrete example is worth more than a page of adjectives. Only publish results and reviews that are real and that you have permission to show.

A quick test: show the page to someone outside your business for ten seconds, then ask what you sell, who it is for and what they should do next. If they cannot answer all three, visitors cannot either.

5. Remove the reasons to hesitate

Before a person writes to you, small doubts pile up: "Will they reply?", "Is there a hidden fee?", "What if I am not happy?" A short FAQ block handles these directly. Good questions come from your real inbox: what customers ask before they book. Mention response time ("we reply within one working day"), how payment works, and what happens if something goes wrong. Keep the answers short and honest.

6. Make the next step easy, and repeat it

Put the call to action at the top, after the proof and at the bottom, and keep it the same each time so nobody has to guess. Offer more than one way to reach you, because people differ: a phone number that works with one tap on mobile, a messenger link and a short form with only the fields you really need. Make sure the contact details are visible without scrolling on a phone. If you are not tracking where enquiries come from yet, set that up too; our guide on tracking website leads shows how.

7. Don't forget the technical basics

A page can be perfectly written and still underperform. Give it a clear title and description that match how people search, a single H1 heading, descriptive image names and alt text, and links to related services and to your Google Business Profile article if you serve a local area. Check that it loads quickly on mobile, since most visitors arrive on a phone. Add each service page to your sitemap and link to it from the main menu so both visitors and Google can find it.

A simple structure you can reuse

  • Headline: the service, the audience and the main result
  • One clear button and a phone number
  • What is included (and what is not)
  • Who it is for and where you work
  • Price or price range, and what affects it
  • Process in 3 to 5 numbered steps
  • Proof: a real case, reviews, certificates
  • Short FAQ
  • Final call to action with a form
