Google Business Profile: get local customers

August 24, 2026·7 min read

If someone nearby searches "plumber near me" or "hair salon [your city]", what they see first is not a website — it's the map with three listings and a handful of stars underneath. That box is your Google Business Profile (formerly Google My Business), and for a huge share of local searches it decides who gets the call before anyone even clicks through to a site. It's free, it takes an afternoon to set up properly, and most small businesses still get it half wrong.

1. What Google Business Profile actually is

It's the free listing that shows your business on Google Maps and in local search results: name, category, address, hours, phone, photos, reviews and posts, all in one panel. Google pulls from it to answer questions like "is this place open now" or "how far is it" without the person ever leaving the search page. A weak or empty profile doesn't just look unfinished — it actively pushes people toward the competitor whose listing answers those questions instead.

2. Claim it and verify it properly

Search your business name on Google. If a listing already exists — created automatically or by an old employee — claim it rather than starting a duplicate; two competing listings for the same business confuses Google and splits your reviews. Verification is usually done by a postcard to your address, a phone call, or email, depending on the business type. Skip this step and Google won't let you edit anything, and the listing stays whatever a stranger last typed into it.

3. The fields that actually decide whether people call you

Most of the profile is optional, but a handful of fields do almost all the work:

  • Primary category — the single most important setting. "Dentist" and "Cosmetic dentist" surface for different searches; pick the one that matches what people actually type, then add secondary categories for everything else you do.
  • Hours, including holidays — nothing loses trust faster than "closed" showing on the map while your door is open, or the reverse.
  • Photos — profiles with real, current photos of the storefront, team and work get far more clicks than ones with none. Replace stock-feeling images with actual photos every few months.
  • Services and products — list them with short descriptions and, where you're comfortable, prices. This is also what feeds Google's AI-generated answers about what you offer.
  • Business description — one short, factual paragraph: what you do, who it's for, what makes you different. No slogans, no filler.
Quick test: search your own business on your phone right now. If you can't tell from the listing alone whether you're open, what you actually do, and how far away you are, a stranger can't either — and they'll tap the listing next to yours instead.

4. Reviews: ask for them, answer all of them

Reviews are the single biggest trust signal on the listing, and the count and recency matter almost as much as the star average. Ask satisfied customers directly — in person, by text, or with a link after the job is done; most people are happy to leave one if asked, and almost nobody does it unprompted. Then reply to every review, good or bad. A short, calm, specific reply to a bad review often does more for trust than ten five-star reviews with no response, because it shows a real business is behind the listing and takes complaints seriously.

5. Posts and updates keep the profile alive

Google Business Profile lets you publish short posts — an offer, a new service, a seasonal update, a photo from a recent job. They show directly in the listing and give Google fresh, dated signals that the business is active. A profile that hasn't been touched in a year reads to both Google and visitors as possibly out of business; a few posts a month keep it visibly alive with very little effort.

6. Common mistakes that quietly cost you customers

  • Business name stuffed with keywords ("Smith Plumbing - Best Cheap Plumber City") — against Google's rules and can get the listing suspended.
  • Wrong or inconsistent address and phone number across the web, which confuses both customers and Google's ranking.
  • No service area set for businesses that travel to the customer instead of a fixed storefront.
  • Letting messages and questions on the listing go unanswered for weeks.
  • Treating it as a one-time setup instead of something to update as hours, services or photos change.

The checklist

  • Listing claimed and verified, not left auto-generated
  • Primary category matches what customers actually search
  • Hours accurate, including holidays and exceptions
  • Real, current photos — storefront, team, work
  • Services and a plain-language description filled in
  • A steady stream of reviews, all of them answered
  • Posts published at least monthly
  • Name, address and phone consistent with your website
