When to Redesign Your Website: 7 Clear Signs

October 4, 2026·8 min read

A website is not a poster you print once. Sooner or later it starts to lag behind the business: the offer changes, customers move to phones, the design begins to look a few years old. The hard question is when to act. Redesigning too early wastes money on something that only needed a tune-up; waiting too long means quietly losing enquiries to competitors whose sites are simply easier to use. This guide helps you tell the difference, and shows how to rebuild a site without throwing away what already works.

1. A redesign is not the same as a repair

Many problems that make owners say "we need a new site" can be fixed on the existing one: a slow page, a weak headline, a form that is hard to find, missing contact buttons on mobile. A redesign is the right call when the foundation itself is the problem: the structure, the platform, or the way the site presents your business. Before you commit to either, write down exactly what is wrong. "It looks old" is a feeling; "visitors leave the pricing page without a single click" is something you can act on.

2. Seven signs it's time to rebuild

It doesn't work well on a phone

If text is tiny, buttons are hard to tap or the menu overlaps the content, you are failing most of your visitors. For local businesses the majority of traffic comes from phones, often from people ready to call right now.

It loads slowly and nobody can fix it

Speed problems caused by a heavy theme, dozens of plugins or an outdated platform are often cheaper to solve with a rebuild than with endless patches.

It describes a business you no longer run

New services, a new target customer, a new location or a new price level: if the site tells the old story, visitors get the wrong idea or contact you about things you don't want to do.

It brings visitors but not enquiries

Steady traffic with almost no calls or forms usually points to a message or structure problem. Sometimes a few edits are enough; sometimes the page order and the whole offer need rethinking.

Updating it is painful

If changing a phone number or adding a service means calling a developer and waiting two weeks, the site will simply stop being updated, and an outdated site is worse than a small one.

It is missing the basics visitors expect

  • HTTPS and no browser security warnings
  • Clear contact options: phone, WhatsApp, a short form
  • Prices or at least a clear "how it works and what it costs"
  • Real photos, reviews and company details
  • A privacy policy and a working cookie notice

You are embarrassed to send people the link

This is subjective, but telling. If you hesitate to give the address to a new client, the site is already costing you trust.

3. Signs you only need a tune-up

Not every old-looking site needs replacing. Leave the structure alone and fix individual parts if the site is reasonably fast, the platform is current, the content is mostly accurate and enquiries arrive at a steady rate. In that case, update the photos, refresh the headline and calls to action, check the forms, and see whether results improve before spending more. A good rule: change one thing, wait a few weeks, look at the numbers.

Before any redesign, save the numbers: monthly visits, enquiries, and your most visited pages. Without a "before" picture you cannot tell afterwards whether the new site is actually better.

4. What to keep when you rebuild

The worst redesigns throw everything away. Keep what has proven itself:

  • Pages that rank in Google. Check Search Console for pages that already bring visitors, and keep their topics and, where possible, their addresses.
  • Reviews and case studies. They are your strongest proof and cost nothing to carry over.
  • Texts that work. If a paragraph already convinces people, don't rewrite it only because the design changed.
  • Your domain name. Years of history behind the address are valuable; change it only for a very good reason.

5. How to avoid losing Google positions

A redesign that changes page addresses without redirects can erase years of search visibility in a few days. Make a list of all current addresses before the work starts. For every address that changes, set up a permanent (301) redirect to the new equivalent. Keep titles and main headings on important pages close to the originals, submit the new sitemap in Google Search Console, and check for errors during the first weeks. Expect a small dip while Google re-reads the site; it should recover if the migration was done carefully.

6. A realistic process

  • Goal and numbers. What should the site do: bring calls, bookings, orders?
  • Content audit. What stays, what is rewritten, what is removed?
  • Structure first, design second. Agree on the pages and the path to the enquiry before choosing colours.
  • Build and test on phones. Check forms, speed and every contact button.
  • Launch with redirects and tracking in place.
  • Review after a month and adjust based on real data.

A good rebuild does not promise a miracle; it removes obstacles between a visitor and an enquiry, and results come from how well your offer is presented and promoted afterwards.

Checklist before you decide

  • I can name the specific problem the new site must solve
  • I have saved current visits and enquiry numbers
  • I know which pages bring visitors from Google
  • I have ruled out cheaper fixes: speed, headline, forms
  • The plan includes redirects, mobile testing and tracking
