Facebook & Instagram Ads for Small Business

September 7, 2026·7 min read

A lot of small business owners try Facebook or Instagram ads once, spend a few hundred euros, get little to show for it, and conclude that "ads don't work for us". In most cases the platform isn't the problem — the setup is. Meta's ad tools are built to handle large budgets and constant testing, and left on default settings they will happily spend a modest budget showing the wrong offer to the wrong people. Here's what needs to be right before you spend a cent, and what tends to go wrong when it isn't.

1. Google Ads or Meta Ads — they solve different problems

Google Ads catches people who are already searching for what you sell — the demand exists, you're just competing to be found. Facebook and Instagram work the opposite way: nobody is searching, so the ad has to create interest in someone who wasn't looking for you a moment ago. That makes Meta ads better suited to visual products and services, local awareness, and offers that are easy to understand in three seconds of scrolling. It makes them a poor fit for anything that needs research or comparison before someone acts. Many businesses eventually run both, but they answer different questions — don't judge one by the standard of the other.

2. Pick the objective that matches the goal, not the biggest button

Ads Manager offers objectives like Awareness, Traffic, Engagement, Leads and Sales, and the platform optimizes ruthlessly for whichever one is selected. Choose Traffic and it will find people who click cheaply — not people who buy. Choose Engagement and it will chase likes and comments, which look good in a report and change nothing about revenue. For most small businesses the objective should be Leads (form fills, calls, messages) or Sales, because those are the only two that tell the algorithm to optimize for people who actually take the action that pays the bills.

3. Targeting: start narrow, not broad

For a local business, geographic targeting matters more than almost anything else in the setup — a radius around the service area, not a whole country. Beyond location, resist the urge to stack ten unrelated interests hoping to widen reach; a smaller, relevant audience nearly always outperforms a broad one in the first weeks, because the algorithm has less noise to sort through. Once a campaign has enough data — real leads or purchases, not just clicks — a lookalike audience built from that data usually outperforms interest-based targeting built from guesses.

4. Creative that stops the scroll

The creative matters more on Meta than the targeting does, because the ad is competing with photos from friends and family in the same feed, not with other ads in a search results list.

  • Shot on a phone beats a studio photo — polished stock imagery reads as an ad and gets scrolled past; something that looks like a real post gets a second look.
  • Show the product, service or team, not a logo or a slide full of text.
  • Text overlay carries the message — a large share of video is watched with the sound off.
  • One offer per ad. An ad trying to say five things at once says nothing clearly.
  • Refresh creative every couple of weeks — the same audience seeing the same ad too often stops noticing it, and cost per result climbs.
Quick test: scroll your own Instagram feed for two minutes and count how many ads you actually stopped for. Almost none will be polished studio photography — most will look like something a real person posted. That's the bar your ad creative needs to clear, not a brochure.

5. Budget: how much, and how it actually gets spent

A new campaign goes through a "learning phase" where the algorithm is still figuring out who to show the ad to, and results during that window are usually worse and more expensive than they'll be once it settles. Turning a campaign off after three days because the cost per lead looked high is one of the most common ways small budgets get wasted — the data never had a chance to mature. Start with a budget you're comfortable testing for at least one to two weeks without touching it, even a modest one, rather than a larger budget you'll panic and pause after two days.

6. Tracking: without the pixel, you're flying blind

The Meta Pixel (or the newer Conversions API) installed on the website tells the algorithm which visitors actually became a lead or a sale, not just who clicked. Without it, Meta can only optimize for clicks — a much weaker signal — because it has no way to learn who converted after landing on the site. Setting this up before launch, and testing that a form submission actually fires the event, is a five-minute step that has an outsized effect on how efficiently a budget gets spent.

7. Common mistakes that burn budget fast

  • Boosting a post instead of building a real campaign in Ads Manager — boosted posts have far fewer targeting and optimization options.
  • Sending traffic to the homepage instead of a page that matches the exact offer in the ad, which loses people who came for one specific thing.
  • Editing the campaign daily during the learning phase, which resets the learning and keeps costs elevated.
  • No frequency check — showing the same ad to the same small audience so often that people start ignoring or hiding it.
  • No tracking, which turns every decision about what's working into a guess instead of a fact.

Checklist before you launch your first campaign

  • Objective set to Leads or Sales, not Traffic or Engagement
  • Geographic targeting matches where the business actually serves customers
  • Pixel or Conversions API installed and tested with a real form submission
  • Landing page matches the ad's specific offer, not just the homepage
  • Creative shot recently, not stock photography, with one clear message
  • Budget and patience set for at least one to two weeks before judging results
