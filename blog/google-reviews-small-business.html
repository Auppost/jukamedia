Get More Google Reviews for Your Business

September 16, 2026·7 min read

Before a customer calls, books or walks in, they check your Google reviews — often before they even open your website. A business with 60 reviews at 4.8 stars beats a nearly identical competitor with 6 reviews at 4.2, even if the second business is objectively just as good. Reviews aren't a vanity metric on the side of your Google Business Profile; for most local searches, they're the first thing a stranger uses to decide whether to trust you at all.

1. Reviews decide who gets picked, not just who ranks

Google's local results — the map and the three-listing "local pack" above them — weigh review count and rating heavily alongside relevance and distance. But the bigger effect happens after ranking, in the customer's head: two businesses sitting side by side in search results, and the one with more recent, positive reviews gets the click and the call. A strong review profile doesn't just help you get found — it's often the actual deciding factor once you already have.

2. Ask at the right moment, not weeks later

The best time to ask for a review is right after a customer tells you, unprompted, that they're happy — right after a job well done, a delivery that arrived on time, or a service that solved their problem. Waiting for a follow-up email a week later means asking someone who has already moved on mentally; the moment passes and the review never gets written. Build the ask into a moment that already exists in your process: the end of a service call, the checkout message, the thank-you text after a job finishes.

3. Make it a one-tap link, not a scavenger hunt

Every extra step between "yes, I'll leave a review" and the review actually being posted loses you a chunk of people who meant it sincerely. Get your direct Google review link from your Google Business Profile dashboard (under "Get more reviews" or "Ask for reviews") and put it everywhere: a QR code on the counter or receipt, a link in the WhatsApp or SMS thank-you message, a button in your email signature. Asking someone to "search us on Google and leave a review" instead of handing them a direct link is the single biggest reason well-intentioned customers never follow through.

4. Reply to every review — yes, even the one-star one

A reply signals to every future visitor reading that review that a real person runs this business and pays attention. Thank positive reviewers by name and mention something specific from their visit; it takes thirty seconds and reads as genuine rather than templated. For a negative review, reply calmly, acknowledge the specific complaint without getting defensive, and say what you did or will do about it — that reply is written for the hundred people who'll read it later, not just the one person who left it.

A simple reply template for a negative review: "Thanks for the honest feedback, [name]. We're sorry the [specific issue] didn't meet what you should expect from us. We've [fixed / are looking into] this, and we'd like the chance to make it right — please reach out at [phone/email]." Calm, specific, no arguing in public.

5. What not to do: gating, buying, or review swaps

"Review gating" — asking customers privately how their experience was and only directing the happy ones to leave a public review — violates Google's policies and can get your whole profile suspended if reported. The same goes for buying reviews, running "leave us 5 stars and get 10% off" incentives, or trading reviews with other business owners. These tactics produce a review profile that looks fine for a while and then collapses all at once when Google's spam detection catches up, which is far worse for trust than a slower, honest climb.

6. When a review is genuinely fake or against policy

Not every unfair review has to just sit there. If a review is from someone who was never a customer, contains hate speech or spam, or is clearly posted by a competitor, you can flag it for removal from the review itself or your Google Business Profile dashboard. Google does remove a share of flagged reviews, but it's slow and not guaranteed — don't count on it as your main defense. A calm, professional public reply that a fair-minded reader can see does more for your reputation than a removal request that may never resolve.

7. Put your best reviews to work elsewhere

A five-star review sitting only on your Google profile is doing a fraction of the work it could. Screenshot standout reviews (with the customer's name, blurred if needed) for your website's testimonials section, your Instagram stories, and your Google or Facebook ad creative. A real quote from a real local customer, tied to a specific result, consistently outperforms generic marketing copy — and it costs nothing beyond the ten minutes it takes to collect it.

8. Watch the trend, not just the average

Your overall star average moves slowly and can hide a real problem. What matters more day to day is your review velocity — how many new reviews you're getting per month — and whether recent reviews are trending up or down compared to older ones. A sudden run of 3-star reviews after months of 5-star ones is an early warning that something changed in your service, pricing or staff, and it's worth investigating before the average catches up and starts costing you customers.

Checklist for a review system that runs itself

  • A direct Google review link, saved somewhere your whole team can grab it in one click
  • A specific, repeatable moment in your process where you ask — not a vague "leave us a review sometime"
  • Every review answered within a few days, positive and negative alike
  • No gating, incentives, or review swaps — ever, even when a competitor is doing it
  • A monthly check of new review count and rating trend, not just the all-time average
  • Your best reviews reused on your website, social media and ads
