Cookie Banner and GDPR for Your Website

October 13, 2026·8 min read

Sooner or later every business owner with a website asks the same question: "Do I really need that cookie banner?" Some owners ignore the topic until a complaint arrives; others install the first plugin they find and end up with a banner that annoys visitors and still doesn't do what the law expects. The truth sits in the middle. If you sell to people in the EU, a few basic privacy habits are part of running a website, and they are much easier to set up at the start than to patch later. (This article is practical guidance, not legal advice. For anything unusual, ask a lawyer.)

1. What GDPR actually asks of a small website

GDPR is the EU's data protection regulation, and it applies to any site that collects personal data from people in the EU, whatever the size of the business. In practice, for a small business website that comes down to a short list: tell people what you collect and why, collect only what you need, get consent where consent is required, keep the data safe, and let people ask what you hold about them. Nothing here requires a legal department, but each point needs to be visible on the site rather than assumed.

2. Which cookies need consent, and which don't

Cookies fall into two groups. Strictly necessary cookies keep the site working: remembering a language choice, keeping a session open, protecting a form from spam. These don't need consent. Everything else does: analytics that track visitors across pages, advertising pixels, remarketing tags, and embedded tools that set their own cookies. The rule of thumb is simple: if a cookie is there for your benefit (measuring, advertising) rather than to deliver the page the visitor asked for, ask first, and don't load it until the visitor says yes.

3. What a compliant cookie banner looks like

A banner that merely says "We use cookies" with a single OK button usually isn't enough. A workable one has a few things in common:

  • A real choice — "Accept" and "Reject" given equal visual weight, not a bright button next to a hidden grey link
  • Categories — necessary, analytics and marketing, so visitors can accept some and refuse others
  • Nothing fires early — analytics and ad scripts load only after consent, not before and not "while the banner is showing"
  • Easy to change — a permanent "Cookie settings" link in the footer so people can withdraw consent as easily as they gave it
  • Plain language — a short explanation and a link to your privacy policy, not a wall of legal text

4. Forms, email and everything else that collects data

Cookies get the attention, but the contact form is where most small sites collect the most personal data. Ask only for what you need to reply: a name and a way to reach the person is usually enough, and every extra required field lowers the number of enquiries anyway. Next to the submit button, say in one line what happens to the data and link to your privacy policy. If you want to send newsletters, use a separate, unticked checkbox, since a person sending an enquiry hasn't agreed to a mailing list. The same logic covers chat widgets, booking tools and any third-party service that receives the visitor's details.

A quick test: open your site in a private browser window, decline everything on the banner, and look at the network activity in the browser's developer tools. If Google Analytics or an advertising pixel still sends data, your banner is decoration and your set-up needs fixing.

5. Analytics and ads without losing your data

The usual fear is that a banner will wipe out your statistics. Some visitors will decline, and that is their right, but the picture is less bleak than it sounds. Google's consent mode lets Google Analytics and Google Ads adjust their behavior based on the visitor's choice and estimate part of the missing conversions in aggregate. Combine that with clean tracking of form submissions and calls (see our guide on tracking leads from your website) and you keep a usable picture of what's working, without tracking people who said no.

6. The privacy policy: short, specific, findable

A privacy policy copied from another company's site is a common problem, because it describes tools and practices you may not use at all. A good one is specific to you: who you are and how to contact you, which data you collect (form details, analytics, ad tools), why, who processes it for you (hosting, email, analytics provider), how long you keep it, and how a person can ask for access or deletion. It should be linked from the footer of every page and next to every form. Keep it up to date when you add a new tool.

7. Hosting, security and third-party tools

Privacy is also about how the site is built. Use HTTPS everywhere, keep the site software updated, and avoid loading things you don't need. Every extra script, font service or embedded widget is another third party that may receive your visitors' data, so fewer of them means less to declare and less to go wrong. A lean site built with only the scripts it needs, as described in our website maintenance plan, makes compliance much simpler than a heap of plugins.

Checklist for a privacy-friendly website

  • Cookie banner with equal "Accept" and "Reject" options and separate categories
  • Analytics and advertising scripts that load only after consent
  • A "Cookie settings" link in the footer to change or withdraw consent
  • A privacy policy written for your actual business and linked on every page
  • Forms that ask only for what you need, with a short data notice by the button
  • A separate, unticked checkbox for any newsletter sign-up
  • HTTPS, updated software and a short list of third-party tools you can name
