Website Maintenance After Launch: A Simple Plan

October 7, 2026·8 min read

Launching a website feels like crossing a finish line, but it is closer to opening a shop. Doors open, and from then on someone has to keep the lights on, restock the shelves and notice when something breaks. Most small business sites are not damaged by a dramatic attack. They decay quietly: a contact form stops sending, a plugin goes out of date, a phone number changes on the business card but not on the site. This guide gives you a plain maintenance plan you can follow in about an hour a month, and explains what is worth handing to someone else.

1. What "maintenance" actually means

Maintenance is not redesign and it is not marketing. It is the routine work that keeps the site working, accurate and safe. In practice it covers five areas:

  • Technical health: software updates, backups, hosting and domain renewals, security certificate.
  • Working parts: forms, buttons, chat, phone and WhatsApp links, booking tools.
  • Content accuracy: prices, opening hours, services, team, contact details.
  • Speed and mobile experience: pages that stay fast as content is added.
  • Tracking: analytics and conversion events that still record what you think they record.

2. The monthly check (about an hour)

Test every way a customer can contact you

Submit your own contact form and confirm the message arrives, and not in a spam folder. Tap the phone number and the WhatsApp button on your phone. This single test catches the most expensive failure: a site that looks fine while enquiries quietly disappear.

Click through the main pages on a phone

Open the home page, your key service pages and the contact page. Look for broken images, overlapping text, links that lead nowhere and pop-ups that cannot be closed.

Check speed

Run your main pages through a speed tool once a month and compare with last month. Heavy new photos or an added script are the usual culprits. Our guide on website speed for small business explains what to look at.

Look at the numbers

Open your analytics and compare visits and enquiries with the previous month. You are not looking for perfection, only for sudden drops that tell you something broke.

The most useful habit is the simplest one: put a recurring reminder in your calendar and test your own contact form on the same day every month. A form nobody tests is a form that eventually stops working.

3. The quarterly and yearly list

  • Every quarter: review and apply software and plugin updates, after taking a fresh backup; remove accounts of people who no longer work with you; check that your pages still appear in Google for your brand name.
  • Every year: renew the domain and hosting well before the expiry date, review all texts and prices, replace outdated photos, check the privacy policy and cookie notice against what the site actually does.
  • After any business change: new address, new phone, new service or new price means the site is updated the same week, together with your Google Business Profile.

4. Backups: the part nobody wants to think about

A backup is only useful if you can restore from it. Ask three questions: where are the copies stored, how often are they made, and has anyone ever tried restoring one? Keep at least one copy outside the hosting account itself. For a small site, a daily or weekly automatic backup kept for a month is usually enough. Make a fresh copy manually before any major change.

5. Security basics that cover most small sites

  • Use a unique, long password for hosting, the website admin and your domain account, ideally stored in a password manager.
  • Turn on two-step verification wherever it is offered.
  • Keep the platform and plugins updated, and delete the ones you don't use.
  • Give each person their own login instead of sharing one, and remove access when a freelancer finishes.
  • Make sure the site uses HTTPS and the certificate renews automatically.

Simpler sites carry less risk. A fast, lightweight site with fewer plugins has fewer things that can go out of date, which is one reason we keep builds lean.

6. Content that goes stale

Visitors judge a business by how current its site feels. Prices from three years ago, a team photo of someone who left, a "news" section last updated in 2022 or a copyright year that stopped moving all create doubt. You don't need to publish constantly. You do need what is on the site to be true. Once a quarter, read the site as if you were a new customer and fix anything that makes you hesitate.

7. Who should do it?

There are three realistic options, and the right one depends on how the site was built and how much time you have.

  • You, with a checklist. Works for the monthly test and for simple text edits, if your site has an editor you are comfortable with.
  • Your developer or agency on a small plan. Best for updates, backups, fixes and speed checks. Ask what is included, how fast they respond and who owns the hosting and domain accounts.
  • A mix. You handle content, a specialist handles the technical side. This is the most common setup for small businesses.

Whatever you choose, make sure you personally hold the logins for your domain and hosting. Losing access to your own domain because a former freelancer owned the account is a painful and surprisingly common problem.

Maintenance checklist

  • Contact form, phone and WhatsApp tested this month
  • Main pages checked on a phone
  • Latest backup exists and is stored outside the hosting
  • Updates applied, unused plugins and accounts removed
  • Prices, hours and contact details match reality
  • Domain and hosting renewal dates are in my calendar
  • Analytics still records enquiries correctly
