Why Your Website Needs Estonian and Russian

September 13, 2026·8 min read

Most small business sites in Estonia launch in one language and stop there. If that language is English or only Estonian, a large share of the local market never gets past the homepage — not because they aren't interested, but because they can't quickly tell what you sell, what it costs, or how to reach you. Estonia's customer base naturally splits across Estonian, Russian and, for tourists and expats, English. A site that only speaks one of those is quietly filtering out paying customers before they ever pick up the phone.

1. Estonia's language reality most sites ignore

Estonia has a large Russian-speaking population, concentrated most heavily in Tallinn and Ida-Virumaa but present everywhere in the country, alongside Estonian speakers and a growing number of English-speaking residents, remote workers and tourists. A business serving Tallinn, Narva or any mixed-population area is realistically serving all three groups at once. Assuming everyone reads Estonian, or defaulting to English because it feels international, both leave real, nearby customers unable to actually understand your offer.

2. "Multilingual" means more than a translate button

A browser auto-translate widget bolted onto a single-language site is not the same thing as a multilingual website, and customers can tell the difference immediately. Machine-translated navigation menus, mangled service names and prices that render oddly after translation all read as unfinished and unprofessional — the opposite of the trust a small business needs to win a first-time customer. A real multilingual site has separate, properly written pages for each language, translated by someone who understands both the language and the business, not just run through a translator at the last minute.

3. Each language needs its own URL, not a toggle

Search engines index languages as separate pages, not as a JavaScript toggle applied on top of one page. A site with real, separate URLs per language — like /blog/ for English, /ru/blog/ for Russian and /et/blog/ for Estonian on this very site — lets each version get indexed and rank on its own in Google's results for that language, instead of competing for a single ranking that only ever shows one language to everyone. hreflang tags then tell Google which version to show a Russian-language searcher versus an Estonian-language one, so the right audience lands on the right page automatically.

4. Where to start if you can't translate everything at once

A full trilingual rebuild is not always realistic for a small budget, and it doesn't need to happen in one step. Start with the pages that carry the actual buying decision: the homepage, your main service or product pages, pricing, and the contact or booking form. A visitor who can read your offer and your price in their own language is far more likely to convert than one who has to guess, even if your blog or about page is still only in one language for now.

Quick test: open your site on a phone, switch it to a language other than the one you built it in first, and try to find your price and phone number without knowing the original language. If you can't do it in a few seconds, a real customer in that language can't either.

5. Keep business details identical across every language

Your business name, address, phone number and opening hours need to read exactly the same on the Estonian, Russian and English versions of your site and your Google Business Profile. A translated street name, a differently formatted phone number or hours that don't match between language versions don't just look sloppy — they confuse both customers and search engines trying to confirm you're a real, consistent local business. Keep the factual details untranslated and identical; translate only the surrounding text.

6. Common mistakes that undo a multilingual site

  • Relying on a browser translate widget instead of real pages. It's free, but it produces stilted text, breaks on product names and prices, and search engines don't index the translated version at all.
  • A language switcher that's hard to find. If it's buried in a footer or missing from mobile view, visitors give up before they find their language.
  • Inconsistent information between language versions. A price, phone number or service list that differs between the Estonian and Russian pages erodes trust the moment someone compares them.
  • Mixing languages on one page. A page that's mostly Estonian with a few untranslated Russian or English sentences left in reads as unfinished rather than bilingual.
  • Translating everything except the parts that matter most. A translated blog with an English-only pricing page defeats the purpose — prioritize the pages that actually drive a purchase or a call.

7. Your Google Business Profile should match too

A trilingual website paired with a Google Business Profile that only lists your business description and posts in one language sends mixed signals. Google Business Profile supports a business description and posts written for the language a searcher is using, and reviews left in Russian or Estonian carry just as much weight as ones in English when Google decides who to show for a local search. If your website speaks three languages but your profile speaks one, you're still losing the visitors who find you through Maps or local search first rather than through the site itself.

8. Budget realistically, and don't let one language rot

Adding a language later is normal and often the right call financially — but a language version that gets set up once and never touched again eventually falls behind. Prices change, services get added, and if only the original language gets updated, the other versions quietly start showing outdated information to real customers. Whoever maintains your site needs a simple habit: whenever a price, service or contact detail changes, update it everywhere at once, not just in the language you happen to be looking at that day.

Checklist for a working multilingual website

  • Homepage, key service pages, pricing and contact form available in every language you serve
  • Separate URLs per language, not a script-based toggle over one page
  • hreflang tags linking every language version of each page to the others
  • A visible, easy-to-find language switcher on both desktop and mobile
  • Business name, address, phone number and hours identical across all language versions
  • Real, human-checked translations on pages that carry pricing or a call to action
