Choosing a Web Agency: 7 Questions to Ask Before You Sign

October 10, 2026·8 min read

A new website is one of the bigger purchases a small business makes, and it is hard to judge from the outside. Portfolios look polished, quotes arrive in different shapes, and every agency says it is fast, creative and affordable. The difference shows up months later: who owns the site, who answers the phone when the form stops working, and whether anyone can find you on Google. These seven questions help you compare agencies and freelancers on things that actually matter.

1. Who owns the site, the domain and the code?

You should own your domain, your hosting account and your content from day one, registered in your name, not the agency's. Ask directly: if we part ways in a year, do I get the files and the logins? Some providers keep the domain or build on a closed platform, so leaving means rebuilding from scratch. A good partner puts ownership in writing and is happy to do it.

2. Can I see work for businesses like mine, and talk to a client?

A portfolio shows taste, not results. Ask for two or three projects similar to yours in size and industry, and ask what changed afterwards: more enquiries, better rankings, faster response. A short call with a past client tells you more than any case study. If an agency cannot name a single client who would speak to you, treat that as information.

3. What exactly is included in the price?

Two quotes for "a website" can describe very different things. Ask for a list of deliverables and check it against this:

  • Number of pages and languages, and who writes or edits the text
  • Mobile design, not just a desktop layout that shrinks
  • Basic SEO set-up: page titles, descriptions, sitemap, structured data
  • Contact form, analytics and conversion tracking connected and tested
  • Hosting, SSL certificate and the first period of support
  • How many rounds of revisions are included, and what a further round costs

Cost matters, but a surprise invoice for "extras" hurts more than a higher honest quote. If you are still setting a budget, our guide on what a website costs for a small business gives realistic ranges.

4. What does the process look like, and how long will it take?

A clear process is a good sign: a short discovery conversation, a structure or wireframe for approval, design, build, testing, launch. Ask who your contact person is, how often you will see progress, and what the agency needs from you and by when. Most delays on small projects come from missing texts and photos, so a good agency tells you this upfront and gives a realistic timeline instead of a promise that sounds good.

5. How will the site be found, and how will we know it works?

A beautiful site that nobody visits does not help your business. Ask how the agency handles search visibility, Google Business Profile and speed, and what they will track after launch: calls, form submissions, messages. If the answer is only "we use a modern design", keep looking. You can check the basics yourself with our website conversion checklist.

A simple test: ask each agency what they would do first to bring you customers, not what they would design. The best answers start with your business, your customers and your numbers, not with templates.

6. What happens after launch?

Websites need updates, backups, security patches and the occasional fix. Ask what support is included, how quickly they respond, and what ongoing care costs. A site without maintenance slowly gets slower and less safe, as we explain in our article on website maintenance after launch. Agree on a named contact and a response time rather than a vague "we are always here".

7. Do they say no, and do they avoid big promises?

Be careful with anyone who guarantees first place on Google, a fixed number of sales or results in a week. Nobody controls search rankings or your customers' decisions. Good partners talk about what they can influence: speed, clarity, structure, tracking, and honest expectations. They also tell you when you do not need something, such as a ten-page site when one strong landing page is enough.

Red flags worth walking away from

  • No written contract, or terms that give the agency ownership of your site
  • A price far below everyone else, with no explanation of what is cut
  • No questions about your business, only about your budget
  • Guaranteed rankings, traffic or sales
  • No named person responsible for your project
  • Reluctance to show the staging site before you pay the final invoice
